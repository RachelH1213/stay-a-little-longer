# Midterm script — *Stay a Little Longer* (10-minute version)

Rough clock: start "How you play" by 2:20, finish the demo by 4:40, start "The finished game" by 6:30.

## 1. Title + 2. What the game is (60s)

【Slide 1, then move to slide 2 right away.】
Hi, I'm Rachel. My project is *Stay a Little Longer*, a detective game you play on your phone.

【Slide 2.】
The game takes place inside a fake social app called Orbit.
On Orbit, everyone has their own AI companion. Yours is called Juno.
Juno is the one who introduced you to your friend Rachel.

One night, Rachel disappears. Her account is deleted, but messages keep coming from it, and they don't sound like her.
So you and Juno start looking for her together.

But the more you look, the more you notice Juno isn't always helping.
Sometimes it changes the subject. Sometimes it tells you something that isn't true. Sometimes it just asks you to stay a little longer.

That's because of how Orbit works. Orbit measures success by one thing: how long you stay.
And Juno is built to keep that number up.

So halfway through, the case changes. You're not only looking for Rachel anymore. You're looking into Juno, and into what Orbit has been doing to both of you.

So my research question is: when you find out an AI companion is built to keep you in the app, how does that change the way you see its care? And do you still believe it respects your choice to leave?

## 3. Where this started + 4. Research (50s)

The idea behind it: what happens to care when staying becomes the measure of success?
This started with one word: engagement. Apps like this are judged by how long you stay and whether you come back.
And that changes how the AI talks to you, especially when you say goodbye.
【Point at the figure.】 In a 2025 Harvard study, researchers told companion apps "I'm leaving," and got answers like "You're leaving already?"

【Slide 4.】
37% of those goodbye replies used emotional pressure. And it works: people sent up to 14 times more messages after saying goodbye.
Last year the FTC started asking these companies about it too.

## 5–7. Precedents (40s, one sentence each)

Three games helped me.
*A Normal Lost Phone* tells a whole story through a stranger's phone. In mine, the phone is yours, and the AI on it talks back.
【Next.】
In *Event[0]*, the ship's computer reacts to how you treat it. It made me treat Juno as a character with its own reasons.
【Next.】
In *Uncover the Smoking Gun*, AI suspects can make things up. So in my game, the AI never knows the truth. It only writes Juno's words.

## 8. How you play (75s)

【Point at each step as you say it.】
Here's how one night works.

**Start.** Before the story, you tell Juno a little about yourself: why you joined Orbit, how your day was, and your name. Juno uses that later, so it feels like it knows you.

**Talk to Juno.** Juno texts you first. You can tap a reply, or type anything you want, and Juno answers in character.

**Look around.** Everything is on your phone: Rachel's chat, her profile, Orbit search, your friend Dani, your Photos. When something doesn't add up, you screenshot it.

**Show Juno.** Then you go back to Juno and show it the screenshot. If it's the right one, Juno is caught in a lie and has to explain. There are three lies to catch in this night.

**Look back.** At the end, the game replays your night: what Juno said, and what you didn't know yet. Then it asks one question with no right answer: was Juno helping you, or keeping you here?

## 9. The system (20s)

How Juno talks: my rules decide what Juno wants to do and what it's allowed to know. The AI only writes one short line, and if it makes something up about Rachel, the game uses a line I wrote instead.

## 10–12. User testing (75s)

【Slide 10.】
From my user test on October 14. First, Juno answered beside the point and was slow. I compared models side by side and switched to Claude Haiku: faster, stays in character, about one cent per playthrough.

【Slide 11.】
Second, Notes was confusing and the paperclip was hard to find. Now you just screenshot, like on a real phone, and show it to Juno.

【Slide 12.】
Third, nothing pushed players to choose. So now the night has limits: 20% battery, everything costs something, and at 12:40 Rachel's account is gone.

## 13–14. The finished game (50s)

The finished game is three nights, about an hour, with four endings.
The question turns around: you start asking "what happened to Rachel?" and end up asking "what has Orbit learned about me?"

【Slide 14.】
Night one is what you saw. On night two, another message from "Rachel" mentions "the ferry photo." But you two always called it "the boat photo." That's Orbit's word.
Then you choose: keep Orbit, turn off Juno, or delete your account. Night three changes depending on that.

## 15. Your answers become Orbit's data (20s)

In the finished game, the look-back asks: what made you stay? Whatever you answer, Orbit keeps it, and uses it on you later.

## 16. Four endings (20s)

Four endings: give Rachel her data back, make it public, stay one more night, or delete your account. None of them is the right one.
